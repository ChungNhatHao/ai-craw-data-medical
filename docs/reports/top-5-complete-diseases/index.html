Pulmonary hypertension

Raw disease JSON

Aliases

  • Primary pulmonary hypertension
  • PPH
  • Secondary pulmonary hypertension
  • Idiopathic pulmonary hypertension

Summary

Hypertension in the pulmonary circuit; may be primary idiopathic, or secondary to pulmonary or cardiac disease. In this condition pressure is increased in the pulmonary arteries. This causes increased resistance to the blood flow in the pulmonary arterial circulation.

Causes

  • Idiopathic pulmonary hypertension: Increased pulmonary artery pressure and pulmonary vascular resistance without an obvious cause. A genetic basis for primary pulmonary hypertension appears likely. Familial primary pulmonary hypertension occurs in approximately 7% of cases, the pattern of inheritance identified as autosomal dominant.
  • Secondary pulmonary hypertension: Secondary pulmonary hypertension can result from left sided cardiac disease, low oxygen levels, sleep apnoea, thromboembolic diseases and lung disease, e.g. COPD and Interstitial lung disease. It can also arise from intracardiac shunts in congenital heart disease.

Risk factors

  • essential hypertension
  • human immunodeficiency virus
  • portal hypertension
  • collagen vascular disease
  • congenital shunts resulting in increased pulmonary blood flow

Symptoms

  • shortness of breath with exertion
  • general fatigue
  • Syncope
  • dizziness
  • ankle or leg swelling
  • chest pain or pre-cordial pressure

Diagnosis

  • Cardiological report with follow-up
  • Echocardiography (transthoracic or transoesophageal)
  • Resting ECG
  • Blood pressure

Treatment

  • Thromboendarterectomy
  • Sildenafil intravenous prostacyclin
  • high-dose calcium blockers
  • anticoagulation
  • diuresis
  • lung transplantation

Prevention

Not provided by source

Prognosis

The course of PPH is often one of steady deterioration and reduced life expectancy; non-transplanted patients have a one year survival of 68 - 77%, and a five year survival of 22 - 38%.

Aliases

Summary

The elevation of the systolic and/or the diastolic blood pressure. Hypertension is one of the classic risk factors for vascular disease. Persistent hypertension can result in complications in various organs. Additional risk factors (hyperlipidaemia, diabetes mellitus, smoking, overweight) increase the probability of complications. Hypertension can be essential or secondary.

Causes

  • Primary, essential or idiopathic hypertension: Various factors appear to play a role: Heredity, environmental factors (salt intake, obesity, occupation, family size, crowding), metabolic disturbances.
  • Secondary hypertension: Renal disorders (renal hypertension), hormonal disturbances (endocrine hypertension e.g. Cushing’s syndrome), coarctation of the aorta.

Risk factors

  • hyperlipidaemia
  • diabetes mellitus
  • smoking
  • overweight

Symptoms

  • headaches
  • dizziness
  • fatigue
  • epistaxis
  • nervousness

Diagnosis

  • Blood pressure readings
  • Retinal changes (Keith, Wagener and Barker classification)
  • 24-hour blood pressure monitor recording

Treatment

  • Medication with primary hypertension.
  • With secondary hypertension, medication; medical or surgical treatment of underlying cause.

Prevention

Not provided by source

Prognosis

Depending on cause, degree of severity, response to treatment and complications. Favourable prognosis for successful treatment before onset of complications.

Aliases

  • Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy
  • HOCM
  • HCM

Summary

Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy is the most common inherited cardiomyopathy

Causes

  • This is inherited usually in an autosomal dominant fashion. There are many different genetic mutations that cause this condition and the spectrum of disease severity may be high.

Risk factors

  • A history of sudden death in the family history especially at a young age is an important factor in risk stratification.

Symptoms

  • Palpitations
  • episodes of syncope
  • faintness
  • breathlessness

Diagnosis

  • Cardiological report with follow-up
  • Cardiac CT / MRI or coronary angiography (invasive)
  • Echocardiography (transthoracic or transoesophageal)
  • Resting ECG
  • Exercise ECG
  • Blood pressure

Treatment

  • Betablockers are used to reduce the heart rate.
  • Restriction of the exertion of the individual is advised to keep the pulse below 120 beats per minute.
  • An implantable defibrillator may be used to prevent or reduce the risk of Ventricular Fibrillation.
  • Ablation of the muscle bulk in the interventricular septum may reduce muscle mass but the risk of arrhythmias remain.

Prevention

Not provided by source

Prognosis

There is a wide range of severity and each case deserves careful assessment. The prognosis depends on the ventricular wall dimensions, the degree of outflow obstruction as measured by the gradient within the left ventricle, the occurrence of any arrhythmias and any reported sudden death in the family history.

Aliases

Summary

The irreversible death of heart muscle cells due to ischaemia. The ischaemia is caused by the occlusion of one or more branches of the coronary arteries (ischaemic myocardial necrosis) usually by thrombosis related to atheromatous plaque.

Causes

  • Coronary artery disease (CAD)
  • coronary artery spasm
  • inflammatory vascular disease of the coronary arteries

Risk factors

  • Raised cholesterol
  • smoking
  • diabetes
  • elevated blood pressure

Symptoms

  • Severe crushing chest pain
  • weakness
  • sweating
  • nausea
  • vomiting
  • anxiety

Diagnosis

  • Cardiological report with follow-up
  • Cardiac CT / MRI or coronary angiography (invasive)
  • Echocardiography (transthoracic or transoesophageal)
  • Resting ECG
  • Exercise ECG
  • Blood pressure

Treatment

  • Intensive care
  • coronary artery bypass surgery
  • percutaneous transluminal balloon angioplasty (PTCA)
  • ASA/Aspirin
  • Betablockers
  • ACE Blockers
  • CSE Blockers

Prevention

Not provided by source

Prognosis

The prognosis depends on several factors: the degree of stenosis of the coronary arteries, the underlying risk factors, the change in these risk factors after diagnosis, the degree of left ventricular hypertrophy and any associated rhythm abnormalities.

Aliases

  • Stress induced cardiomyopathy
  • Apical ballooning syndrome

Summary

This is a condition that is characterized by temporary reduction in systolic function (usually lasting a few weeks) of the apical (and/or mid) segments of the left ventricle resembling the effects of myocardial infarction, but in the absence of ischaemia from obstructive coronary artery disease.

Causes

  • temporary catecholamine excess
  • coronary artery spasm
  • microvascular dysfunction
  • outflow tract obstruction causing ballooning of the left ventricle

Risk factors

  • significant stressful situations or events

Symptoms

  • chest pain
  • breathlessness
  • collapse

Diagnosis

  • Cardiological report with follow-up
  • Cardiac CT / MRI or coronary angiography (invasive)
  • Echocardiography (transthoracic or transoesophageal)
  • Resting ECG
  • Exercise ECG
  • Blood pressure

Treatment

  • Normal resuscitation and supportive therapy may be required

Prevention

Not provided by source

Prognosis

This has a good prognosis in most cases. With complete recovery of ventricular function and the absence of coronary artery disease, the outcome should be good. However it is thought that this can recur in a small number of people in the right circumstances.
